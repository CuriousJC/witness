## Overview

Audra McIver, cofounder of Plan of Attack, organizes this 2015 NGDC talk around 25 practical game-PR tips. The through-line is preparation: understand the game’s strongest appeal, research the people who reach the right audience, and supply material that makes their work easier. Good outreach is a considered match between a game and a recipient, supported by clear assets and a realistic schedule.

The talk covers both public-facing material and the work behind it: shared contact lists, press-release drafting, localized messaging, screenshot selection, preview builds, review timing, creator pitches, community responses, and a compact mobile-launch example. Historical prices, platform assumptions, and campaign lengths are retained as McIver’s examples rather than presented as current market facts.

## Key takeaways

- **Research before sending** Identify genre-relevant writers and creators, understand their work, and focus effort on a small number of strong fits.
- **Prepare assets around the recipient’s needs** A clear first paragraph, concise pitch, curated screenshots, and an accessible press kit reduce the work needed to understand and cover the game.
- **Schedule playable evidence** Allow time for preview outreach and reviews, and make the requested publication timing clear rather than sending a build at the last moment.
- **Sustain a credible conversation** Blogs, videos, community responses, events, and studio stories need planning and genuine material; repeated demands for coverage are not a substitute.

## 1. Decide what makes the game distinctive

Source timestamp: [0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=0s)

McIver begins by asking the team to identify what makes its game stand out in a crowded field. Her exercise is to have team members each write down five distinctive features, compare the results, and select the strongest collective positioning. The exercise is useful because the people building a game may disagree about what is most important or assume its appeal is obvious to outsiders.

Compare the individual lists and pull together the five strongest features to promote. That work supports later decisions about the release, pitch, and assets: the team can present a consistent proposition rather than changing the explanation with every message. She frames the crowded flow of press announcements as the reason clarity matters, not as proof that merely sending a release creates coverage.

## 2. Choose a strong spokesperson

Source timestamp: [2:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=160s)

Select someone who can speak clearly and engagingly about the game. McIver emphasizes charisma and media training: the person who knows the most technical detail is not automatically the person best prepared for an interview. The role is to help journalists and audiences understand the project, while representing the team in a way that makes the conversation worthwhile.

## 3. Let the team contribute across roles

Source timestamp: [3:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=194s)

Small teams need flexibility. McIver advises against copying a convention merely because everyone else follows it: it may not suit this product. She encourages a jack-of-all-trades mentality, with people willing to take on work outside a narrow specialty. Community management, in particular, needs attention rather than being assumed to happen by itself. A designated spokesperson and a team that contributes to communication solve different problems: one provides a confident public representative, while the other supplies enough capacity to keep the work moving.

## 4. Identify the people who influence the intended audience

Source timestamp: [4:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=243s)

The relevant audience may be reached through traditional games media, bloggers, YouTube channels, or Twitch streamers. McIver recommends identifying the people whose readers or viewers are likely to appreciate this particular game. Reach alone is not the whole criterion. A creator’s subject matter and audience interests make the difference between a plausible match and a large mailing list of people unlikely to care.

## 5. Ask whether there is a real news story

Source timestamp: [4:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=285s)

A developer’s excitement does not automatically make an announcement newsworthy. McIver asks teams to think about the editor’s situation: many announcements compete for limited attention, and a recipient needs a reason this one matters. She describes editors receiving roughly 30–80 press releases a day in the context of the talk. That is her historical description of the workload, not a current measured industry average.

The practical question is what has actually happened or become available that the outlet’s audience would find interesting. A clear development, useful reveal, or compelling angle gives the editor something to work with. Sending messages more often cannot compensate for an announcement with no meaningful content.

## 6. Build a researched, shared press database

Source timestamp: [5:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=339s)

Create a contact database before the urgent moment of announcement. McIver suggests gathering appropriate press contacts through industry events and press lists, and by researching who has covered comparable games. A writer who has shown interest in a neighboring genre provides a much more useful lead than an unrelated address found at random.

She recommends a shared document, such as a Google Drive spreadsheet, so the team can collaborate and keep information accessible. Back it up. The database is a working resource for a campaign, not simply a pile of names to blast with the same message. Research should establish relevance and support considered outreach, including identifying the right individual within an outlet.

## 7. Concentrate effort on the strongest prospects

Source timestamp: [7:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=450s)

McIver invokes the 80/20 principle: much of the useful coverage may come from a relatively small share of contacts. Her recommendation is to identify the most promising people and spend disproportionate effort understanding and reaching them, while continuing to consider other relevant contacts. The ratio is a prioritization heuristic in the talk, not a measured prediction of exactly how a given campaign’s coverage will divide. A short list of strong matches can deserve more attention than expanding an untargeted list.

## 8. Draft the press release early and lead with the news

Source timestamp: [8:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=498s)

Write the release before it becomes an emergency. McIver points out that drafting forces the team to clarify the announcement and identify missing information. If nobody can state the news cleanly, that is useful to discover while there is still time to improve the plan.

Use an informative headline and put the key facts in the first paragraph. An editor should not have to search through a page of setup to discover what is being announced. Later paragraphs can supply detail and a quote, but the opening needs to establish why the message exists. The release is a practical information tool whose structure should respect the reader’s time.

## 9. Budget for localized communication where it matters

Source timestamp: [10:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=602s)

McIver recommends considering localization of PR material, especially when targeting European markets. She mentions French, Italian, German, and Spanish, with French and German as priorities when the budget is smaller. Her point is that reaching a market may require more than an English announcement sent to an address in that country.

She gives a historical translation figure of roughly 14–16 cents per word. The captions do not establish the currency well enough to supply one here, and the figure should not be used as a current quote. The durable planning point in her example is that localization needs a budget and lead time; it should not first become a consideration on announcement morning.

## 10. Schedule for the recipient’s working day

Source timestamp: [11:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=679s)

McIver generally favors Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday, arriving in the local morning of the territory being contacted. Mondays can bring accumulated messages, while Friday can lead directly into a weekend. If Monday is necessary, sending early gives the announcement a better chance of fitting into that working day. These are her scheduling preferences rather than guarantees of response.

Think across time zones and local calendars. A working day in one country may be a holiday in another; she uses the United Kingdom’s Boxing Day as an example. The goal is to avoid a preventable mismatch between when the team sends news and when the intended recipient is available to consider it.

## 11. Curate screenshots instead of sending everything

Source timestamp: [13:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=796s)

McIver suggests taking many screenshots and selecting only the strongest: perhaps four or five from a set of fifty. The point is deliberate curation. A still that happens to come from the game is not automatically a useful illustration of its appeal.

She discusses composition in a screenshot example, drawing attention to empty space, the position of the action, and movement within the frame. The inspected snapshot at this point shows her at the podium rather than that screenshot, so the example’s actual visual arrangement has not been independently checked. Her spoken recommendation is to choose images that communicate clearly and give the viewer something interesting to look at.

## 12. Make video concise and show the game

Source timestamp: [14:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=893s)

Video needs the same selection discipline. McIver advises staying below three minutes and generally preferring something shorter, with meaningful game footage introduced promptly. A viewer should not spend a long time watching setup before learning what the game actually offers. This is her advice in the talk, not an experimentally established universal duration.

She is skeptical of spending limited money on impressive CG that does little to explain the game. Actual gameplay can be a better use of a small budget because it gives the recipient material directly relevant to deciding whether the game is interesting. The appropriate balance still depends on the genre, the audience, and how familiar the property already is.

Campaign structure also varies. A PC or console title can support a sequence of feature-focused videos that reveal more over time. A casual mobile title may be better served by a much more compressed explanation and campaign. Her recommendation is to plan the videos around the product rather than automatically reproduce the schedule of a larger, longer campaign.

## 13. Assemble a complete, easily accessible press kit

Source timestamp: [18:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1084s)

A press kit should let a recipient find the materials needed to cover the game without a long exchange of emails. McIver lists screenshots, concept art, previous releases, executive biographies, and a spokesperson’s headshot among the useful contents. She suggests around 10–15 screenshots in the kit overall. That differs from the earlier four-or-five selection for a particular screenshot set or announcement.

Provide both watermarked and clean images where appropriate, and make the kit easy to access through a link in the release. She mentions GamesPress as a hosting or distribution resource in the context of 2015; this reading does not verify its present terms. The central requirement is that the material be organized and available when somebody decides to use it.

## 14. Keep a blog active and listen to the response

Source timestamp: [19:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1181s)

McIver recommends maintaining a studio or game blog, suggesting an update at least every two weeks. A regular cadence gives interested people a continuing view of development and something to return to. It also creates opportunities to learn what the audience responds to, rather than treating communication as a one-way stream of announcements.

Feedback can reveal which features attract attention or which parts of an explanation need work. Her recommendation involves maintaining a conversation with useful content; simply creating a blog and leaving it dormant does not achieve that. The proposed frequency is a working discipline from her talk, not a rule that every team must follow regardless of capacity.

## 15. Write a brief pitch for a specific recipient

Source timestamp: [21:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1276s)

The pitch should be short, direct, and tailored. McIver cautions against a page of pleasantries that delays the reason for writing. Show that the game is relevant to this recipient’s work and make the proposed story easy to understand. The research done before outreach should be visible in the match, rather than in a generic message sent to everyone.

She suggests brainstorming pitches with the team. Different people may identify a stronger angle or a clearer explanation. A personalized message does not need to be long; it needs to make a credible connection between the game and the recipient’s audience.

## 16. Plan a sustainable series of videos

Source timestamp: [22:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1343s)

Regular videos can include developer diaries and other varied formats, but McIver advises planning enough material before starting a series. Repetition without a useful idea becomes difficult to sustain and gives viewers little reason to return. Think about the sequence of subjects, not just the first upload.

Humor can help if it comes naturally to the team and fits the project. It should not be a forced substitute for substance. The broader recommendation is to prepare a continuing format that the team can actually deliver, with enough variety and advance thought to avoid running out of content immediately.

## 17. Monitor the community and make time to respond

Source timestamp: [23:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1414s)

Watch the channels where players and interested people are talking about the game. McIver encourages the team to help answer questions and maintain that relationship instead of assuming someone else will notice every issue. Community work requires time and responsibility. Monitoring is useful only if the team can respond appropriately, and small studios need to account for that work alongside development.

## 18. Offer a polished preview and allow time for outreach

Source timestamp: [24:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1457s)

Preview code need not be the finished game, but the portion being offered should be polished enough to represent it well. McIver distinguishes an unfinished product from an unreliable demonstration: a preview is somebody else’s first practical encounter with the project and should make the intended experience understandable.

She recommends pitching at least a month before the preview code is available. That gives editors time to plan rather than receiving an unexpected build with an immediate demand. The advance relationship and the readiness of the sample support each other; neither an early email nor a build alone guarantees coverage.

## 19. Give reviewers enough time before launch

Source timestamp: [25:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1509s)

For reviews, McIver suggests supplying code one or two weeks before launch and asking for publication under embargo on launch day. That schedule is intended to allow play and writing while coordinating attention around release. An embargo is a proposed publication arrangement, not something this reading assumes every recipient has accepted merely because it appears in an email.

Prioritize outlets or writers who have already shown interest and follow up thoughtfully. Her emphasis is on preparing review opportunities rather than sending code at the last moment and expecting an immediate article. The launch-day concentration is the campaign objective she describes; the timing still has to leave room for the reviewer’s actual work.

## 20. Understand how streamers entertain their viewers

Source timestamp: [26:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1571s)

A streamer is not simply a traditional journalist with a different delivery channel. McIver emphasizes entertainment and recommends watching creators’ videos before pitching them. Understand the format, the person’s style, and what their audience enjoys. That observation should shape why this game would make a worthwhile episode or stream, rather than assuming the same press pitch will suit every kind of coverage.

## 21. Learn from mistakes and recognize what worked

Source timestamp: [26:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1605s)

Campaigns produce both disappointments and successes. McIver recommends learning from mistakes without becoming defensive, while also taking pride in what goes well. The useful habit is to examine what happened and improve the next effort. Humility helps the team absorb feedback; recognizing successful work helps it retain approaches worth repeating.

## 22. Choose events the budget can support

Source timestamp: [27:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1635s)

Trade and consumer events can help people encounter the game directly, but they need a realistic budget. McIver recommends guided demonstrations, where someone helps visitors understand what they are playing and why it matters. An event presence is a production commitment involving money and staff attention, so its usefulness should be considered alongside the team’s ability to make the experience work.

## 23. Plan genuine news beats

Source timestamp: [27:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1663s)

A campaign needs occasions to communicate: meaningful announcements, new material, or developments that give the recipient a reason to revisit the game. McIver’s advice is to identify those beats rather than reach out repeatedly with nothing new. The earlier work on positioning and assets makes each occasion easier to explain, while the requirement for genuine news keeps the schedule from becoming empty repetition.

## 24. Build the studio’s profile as well as the game’s

Source timestamp: [28:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1682s)

Publicity can also make the company and its people better known. McIver mentions speaking opportunities, interviews, and thought leadership as ways to build a studio profile. That visibility may matter for relationships with partners and potential funding, not only immediate game sales. A team can have a broader story worth telling, provided it offers something compelling rather than expecting the studio’s existence alone to be news.

## 25. Make research the foundation of the campaign

Source timestamp: [28:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1718s)

The final numbered tip returns to the repeated instruction: research. Know comparable games, relevant media, the intended audience, and the routes by which that audience learns about games. Doing this work at the beginning prevents the team from reaching the announcement stage without knowing whom to contact or why.

McIver connects a lack of preparation with indiscriminate spam. Editors are busy, and a campaign that has not identified its actual prospects tends to compensate by sending more messages to less appropriate people. Research is therefore not a decorative planning phase; it is what makes the later communication targeted.

## Bonus: a compact mobile-launch sequence

Source timestamp: [29:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWrwVB90FoA&t=1750s)

McIver closes with a mobile example tailored to the event’s audience. She describes a typical mobile campaign as roughly six weeks, compared with six months or more for PC and console. Those are her 2015 planning examples, not current release rules. She allows exceptions for unusually substantial mobile games whose technology or depth might justify preview coverage.

For the typical case she describes, finish the game, submit it, and wait for approval before announcing the release date. Then make review-code availability clear in a note for editors above the published release, and use those codes in targeted review pitches. Ask for reviews timed to launch through an agreed embargo, seeking concentrated attention on the first day.

Follow up more than once when appropriate, but use gentle reminders rather than demands for coverage. Her final test is empathy: consider how the message feels to a busy person receiving it. Research, advance planning, and respect for that person’s work connect all 25 tips and the launch example.
