The overview

This is a personal position statement about making games in an unsettled industry. Procedural Minds worries about oversized budgets and fast imitation of small games, expects AI tools to persist, and explains why he accepts supporting automation while preserving the creative work he enjoys. His central distinction is between the tools he chooses for his own game and those he would use in a paid role.

The pressure of budgets that require exceptional sales

Procedural Minds opens with a pessimistic view of the game industry, pointing to reported studio closures and disappointing large releases. His concern is the relationship between development spending and the audience a game can realistically reach. If a franchise has never sold at the scale required by its next budget, he questions why the team is being committed to that spending in the first place. This is his assessment of the situation, rather than a researched account of each closure or its causes.

He illustrates the problem using a $70 selling price, a hypothetical 30% storefront share, and a requirement to sell roughly ten million copies. The captions render the development budget as both “half a million” and $500,000, which does not fit that sales requirement: those assumptions leave about $49 per copy, and ten million copies would correspond to roughly $490 million before other costs. The budget wording is therefore unresolved; the useful argument is that a very large budget can turn an otherwise substantial audience into an inadequate commercial result. The passage should not be treated as a dependable budget calculation for a specific game.

Why rapid copying worries small developers

The speaker sees a different threat for independent developers: an easily communicated idea can be imitated before the original is released. His example is a game about finding a needle in hay. The premise is immediately understandable, which makes it easy to promote but, in his account, also easy for others to reproduce. He says some developers have become reluctant to release demos because the exposure could help competitors copy them.

He distinguishes an alleged copy of actual game code from another developer implementing a similar premise. He reports that Valve removed a code-level copy in one case, while arguing that imitation of a simple idea is much harder to prevent. These are the video's descriptions of those incidents, not a legal determination about what any particular clone infringes. He expects copies to appear across multiple platforms, rather than disappear if one store acts.

What worries him is the loss of a small developer's traditional progression: make a modest game, earn a little money, and use that return to finance a more ambitious one. He predicts that simple games will remain valuable as learning projects or free releases but become less dependable as income-producing steps. He allows that an unusually fortunate release could still succeed. His forecast is pessimistic, not a claim that every small game is already commercially impossible.

He also imagines Steam responding to abundant releases with increasingly personal recommendations, more like a YouTube home page that tries to surface what each person wants. He is unsure whether that outcome would help enough. The prediction sits alongside his acceptance that a store need not require or prohibit every development tool to address discovery.

Why he expects restrictions to slow copying rather than end it

The commentary briefly extends the insecurity theme to reported console jailbreaks, mentioning PlayStation 5 and Switch. The speaker describes these as further pressure on larger companies. The video does not establish the exact affected hardware, firmware, catalogue, or exploit conditions, so the passage is best understood as part of his perception of industry instability rather than technical guidance about those systems.

He then discusses a reported attempt by Anthropic to prevent its AI from recreating games. Even if that restriction worked perfectly, he reasons, a person could choose another provider. If major commercial providers all adopted similar measures, local models could remain available. In his view this creates an ongoing contest between restrictions and attempts to bypass them, rather than a permanent solution.

For the same reason, he does not expect the collapse of an AI investment boom to restore the previous development landscape. Already distributed local models would still exist, even if they were slower or required more work than a leading hosted system. His practical conclusion is that developers must decide how to act in the presence of these tools instead of planning around their disappearance. This is an argument about persistence and incentives; the video does not test the capabilities of particular models.

Personal work: preserve the parts of making a game that matter to you

The speaker proposes making two separate decisions: what AI use is acceptable in personal work and what is acceptable in paid employment. Within either category, someone may accept particular uses and reject others. He connects this distinction to reports that Epic is moving strongly toward AI, because he uses Unreal Engine and expects the engine's direction to affect his work.

For his own game, he wants the creative result to be made by people. He is comfortable asking AI questions, looking things up, or helping build supporting utilities, such as a tool that reads a data table or manages tasks. His boundary concerns handing over the game-making activities themselves or shipping finished AI art. The distinction is about his chosen workflow and enjoyment, rather than a claim that he avoids AI entirely.

He enjoys building Blueprints and solving the problems of making his game. Automating those activities would remove something he wants to do, so faster production would not automatically be an improvement for him. Where he lacks an artistic skill, he would rather work with an artist, make contacts, and build the project through collaboration. That human relationship is part of the value he wants from the project.

059:52 ↗

Paid work: adapt to the tools a job requires

Professionally, his position is more pragmatic: if a job requires AI, he says he will learn and use it. The employer is paying for a particular kind of work, and he treats that obligation separately from the preferences governing his personal game. That distinction allows him to be selective about his hobby without making the same commitment on behalf of every future employer.

He discusses an anticipated Unreal Engine 6 direction involving Verse, AI-assisted coding, and a feared loss of Blueprints. Those statements are his expectations in the recording, not confirmed engine specifications in this write-up. He describes himself as an artist-oriented technical artist who has struggled with programming documentation, so such a shift would matter to his own strengths. At the same time, he sees a possible role for someone who understands the art and technical work while using assistance with code.

He does not present that possibility as job security. His contract is ending, the job market is difficult in his account, and he may spend a long period without a suitable role or take something in between. The personal/professional split is therefore a way of choosing how to respond under uncertainty, not a confident prediction about the future employment market.

The channel, sponsorship offers, and the price of independence

The speaker acknowledges that he has already made videos about using AI for supporting development tasks, including his data-table reader. He distinguishes these from tutorials built around letting AI make a game. He says he has received many requests for AI collaborations and sponsorships and has accepted none of them. The captions contain a contradictory sentence about wanting to make such videos, but the surrounding explanation consistently describes a desire to preserve the creative work and avoid those collaborations.

His reasons match the boundary for his own game: he likes handmade characters and the chance to support another person's creative work. He does not want the channel's commercial incentives to replace those activities with AI demonstrations. Nevertheless, he does not make an absolute promise that no offer could ever change his mind. He gives an intentionally extraordinary $100,000 offer as an example of money that could buy substantial time to work on his game.

He considers such a sum unrealistic for a channel of his size. His stated intention is to avoid AI sponsorships for the foreseeable future, while acknowledging that a major life event or exceptional offer might alter that decision; he says viewers would know if that happened. Occasional videos about his own supporting tools may continue, but he also says his current backend workflow is largely established, leaving little new to show.

Why the channel is broadening beyond PCG

Fewer procedural content generation videos do not mean he has abandoned PCG. He attributes the lull to a lack of new PCG updates in the Unreal release direction he describes and to the size of his existing tutorial catalogue. He mentions an expected Unreal 5.9 and MCP-related changes, but these are his understanding of announcements at recording time, not independently verified release information.

The channel is consequently covering a broader range of Unreal technical art, such as editor utility widgets. He remains willing to make a PCG video when he finds an interesting idea, a useful new feature, and enough time to investigate it. The change is driven by what he thinks he can still teach usefully, rather than a commitment to repeat the same material on a fixed schedule.

He closes by returning to individual choice. Different developers value different parts of the process and may adopt more or less AI accordingly; his middle position will not satisfy everyone. For himself, the next step is to keep developing his game and save enough to hire artists. He says an artist has already accepted work on a proper main-character concept. That plan makes his boundary concrete: use selected supporting tools, adapt when working for others, and spend his own creative effort and money on a game made with human collaborators.
